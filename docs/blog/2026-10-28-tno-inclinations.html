Today in one paragraph

A 2026 paper in Icarus by Bansal, Brunton, Batygin, Pichierri and Nesvorný asks a question that sits underneath much of the Planet Nine debate: if the Sun was born in a busy star cluster that stirred up the outer Solar System, could Planet Nine's gravity have since smoothed that stirring away, making a violent birth look quiet today? Their computer simulations say no - a Planet Nine cannot tidy up a strongly disturbed population of distant objects into what telescopes actually see. That means the observed calm of the distant Solar System, if it survives better data, still points to a gentle birth environment, whether or not Planet Nine exists.

The question

The Sun almost certainly formed inside a star cluster, and estimates of how roughly nearby stars treated the young Solar System range from gentle to violent. One argument for a gentle birth comes from the distant trans-Neptunian objects (TNOs): small bodies orbiting far beyond Neptune whose orbital tilts (inclinations) are clustered in a surprisingly narrow range. Strong stellar encounters early on should have tilted these orbits much more, so the narrow spread has been used to argue the birth cluster was mild. The loophole this paper tests: Planet Nine, if it exists, constantly reshapes these orbits over billions of years. Could it have erased the fingerprint of a violent birth - taking a wildly tilted population and making it look calm?

What they did

The authors ran two families of N-body simulations, each following thousands of test objects for 4 billion years under Neptune, a range of Planet Nine configurations (masses of 5 to 10 Earth masses, eccentricities 0.2 to 0.5, tilt 20 degrees), the Galactic tide, and passing stars. The first family starts from a population already strongly stirred by birth-cluster encounters (drawn from published cluster simulations), the second from a comparatively quiet population. They then compared the simulated tilt distributions with the real one, measured from 19 known distant TNOs after correcting for where telescopes tend to look. That correction yields an observed intrinsic tilt width of about 12 degrees.

What they found

In the simulations that started strongly stirred, the final tilt spread stayed at 26 to 27.5 degrees, roughly independent of which Planet Nine was used - far wider than the observed 12 degrees, a mismatch the paper quantifies as about a 3-sigma rejection. Planet Nine perturbed these objects but did not vertically confine them. In the quietly-started simulations, the spread stayed low (16 to 19 degrees, statistically compatible with the observations given the small sample), showing Planet Nine also does not over-stir a calm population. The paper reports one further difference: only the quietly-started simulations reproduce the strong alignment of perihelion directions (the orbital-clustering signal Planet Nine was invoked to explain); the strongly-stirred starts wash that alignment out.

Limits the authors state

The paper is careful about what it has not shown. Only the strongest cluster-stirring scenario compatible with the cold classical Kuiper belt was tested, not intermediate ones. The observed tilt width comes from just 19 objects, so its own uncertainty is broad, and the debiasing method carries a known systematic of a few degrees - though the authors note this is much smaller than the roughly 15-degree gap between the strongly-stirred simulations and the data. And if observational biases against finding highly-tilted objects turn out to be severe, the whole argument weakens: the paper frames the choice as "either the birth cluster was mild, or strong stirring happened and survey biases hide it". The expected Rubin Observatory/LSST harvest of distant TNOs should shrink this uncertainty.

What this does and does not mean for Planet Nine

This paper is not evidence for or against Planet Nine's existence - the planet is an ingredient in the simulations, not their output. What it does is close an escape route: Planet Nine cannot launder a violent birth into the calm distant population seen today. It also notes a side-effect relevant to the Planet Nine case itself: in these simulations, a strongly-stirred birth actually weakens the perihelion clustering that Planet Nine is meant to produce, so the Planet Nine scenario pairs more naturally with a quiet birth. An appendix re-checks the separate cold-belt constraint on stellar flybys and reproduces a previously published upper bound on the birth cluster's strength.
