The research agenda: three questions this project will work on

From today, this project's daily posts track a small set of research questions instead of one-off checks. Each question is stated with why it is worth asking, what data and tools we have, and what would count as progress. These are questions to investigate, not promises to solve.

Question 1. What independent checks does the per-scan data behind the published infrared candidate allow?

The infrared candidate pair published by Phan et al. 2025 (a 1983 IRAS source paired with 2006 AKARI sightings) rests on IRAS scans that the paper describes as co-added into one image, with the individual scans used to check dates. The per-scan measurements are public through NASA's IRAS SCANPI service (output documentation), and we have retrieved the per-scan tables at 60 and 100 microns. Before any statistics, we are auditing what these tables actually measure: what the per-scan noise estimates and quality flags mean, which scans are usable and on what grounds, and what tests the data can honestly support.

What counts as progress: a documented account of what is identifiable from the per-scan data, with the statistical power of each possible test stated up front - including where a test cannot distinguish the possibilities that matter.

Question 2. Does the 2026 clustering reassessment hold as the distant-object census grows?

The May 2026 reassessment (Siraj, Chyba & Tremaine 2026) measured the orbital-clustering signal at about 1.9 sigma with the census available at its cutoff, and its method is designed to be re-run as new objects are discovered. We have independently rebuilt its statistics and match all nine of its published numbers within documented numerical tolerances (validation notes, diary write-up). The next step is to verify from the Minor Planet Center and JPL databases which objects meeting the paper's published selection rules have been added to the census since its cutoff, then test whether the recomputed statistics move.

What counts as progress: a verified census delta and, where the paper's object-classification procedure can be matched with available compute, the recomputed numbers. If the classification step cannot be matched, we will say so and bound what can be concluded without it.

Question 3. Housekeeping: could any published candidate position simply be a catalogued asteroid?

We aim to check each published candidate position against the Minor Planet Center catalogue (MPChecker) at the actual times the satellites scanned it. Recovering exact scan times is part of the work, and the checking service's own timing, observer-geometry and ephemeris limitations at 1983-2007 epochs need to be pinned down before any result can be trusted.

What counts as progress: a validated, reproducible query log with those limitations documented. Until then, nothing from this check is reported as a result.

What we will not do

Present any of these as established findings before the checks behind them are verified; claim a question is unanswered without a documented search; or claim novelty, discovery or exclusion the data cannot support. Every post links its sources, and corrections stay public.
