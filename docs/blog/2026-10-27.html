Research diary: 27 October 2026

Plain-English log of what this project actually checked and found today. Technical details and code are linked throughout. Analysis here is by this project and is not peer reviewed.

Today in one paragraph

The site you are looking at stopped being a demo and became a real evidence map. We also ran our first two original tests: we checked four published "possible Planet Nine" infrared sightings against the best-known published model of where the planet should be, and we stress-tested the statistics behind the 2026 evidence. One useful measurement: we quantified how sensitive the 2026 statistics are to exactly where each object is placed at "detection".

1. The map is now real data only

The map on the main page shows where 21,740 simulated planets from the published Brown & Batygin (2021) model would sit in the sky tonight, after removing the ones that published telescope searches (ZTF, DES, Pan-STARRS) say they would already have found. Every number comes from a public source you can check: the model file (CaltechDATA, checksum-verified), the object orbits (JPL SBDB and Horizons), and the published papers. We validated our position calculations against JPL's own ephemeris in the tests we have run so far: typical agreement about 1 arcminute, worst case about 4 arcminutes, over ensemble epoch-reproduction and two-object long-drift checks (a limited benchmark, not a guarantee) - all far finer than the map's 1-degree grid. The old toy demo is gone.

2. Do the published infrared sightings fit the model's map?

Four candidate points have been published from far-infrared satellites: a pair from IRAS (1983) and AKARI (2006/07) by Phan et al. 2025, and two single AKARI detections from a 2025 search. We wound the 100,000-model ensemble back to each sighting's own date and asked: did any model planet sit there then?

Answer: for the IRAS/AKARI pair, the nearest model orbit in the published ensemble is about 21 degrees away at both dates - roughly 100 times the typical spacing between model points, so it lies far outside the region the published model actually covers. For the two single AKARI points, the nearest model orbits are 2-4 degrees away, similar to gaps at the model's own edges, so we can only say "near the fringe", nothing stronger.

What this does not mean: it does not show the sightings are wrong. It compares them against one 2021 model, and the 2026 evidence says that model is itself too narrow. We also initially overstated this as "outside the model"; on re-check we added a calibration (how tightly the 100,000 points actually cover the sky) and softened the wording to match what a finite sample can prove. That correction is part of the public record (technical write-up).

3. Stress test: how precisely does the 2026 method need its inputs?

The original Planet Nine case is that the orbits of distant small bodies point in suspiciously similar directions. A May 2026 reassessment (Siraj, Chyba & Tremaine 2026) found that with the latest object sample the signal is about 1.9 sigma - interesting, not convincing. We rebuilt their method independently and reproduce all nine of their published numbers to within 0.05 sigma (validation notes).

Then we measured how sensitive their statistic is to its inputs. The method places each object where a survey actually detected it. We deliberately moved those input positions along each object's orbit - to the middle and the end of its observed arc, a median shift of about 11 degrees - and measured what changed: on the current sample the headline statistic moved from 1.9 sigma to 1.4, and the best-fit direction from 150 to 178 degrees.

Takeaway, stated carefully: the later positions are synthetic inputs - no telescope detected these objects there - so the shifted numbers are not an alternative answer, and they neither weaken nor strengthen the published 1.9 sigma. We report the measurement itself: these inputs moved by this much, and the outputs changed by that much. Full table, method and limits: research notes.

What we did not do today

No new object discoveries, no telescope time, no claim that Planet Nine does or does not exist. Today's work narrows and quality-checks what published evidence can say. Sources: Siraj, Chyba & Tremaine 2026; Siraj et al. 2025; Phan et al. 2025; AKARI far-infrared search 2025; Brown & Batygin 2021 reference population; MPC distant objects; JPL SBDB/Horizons.
