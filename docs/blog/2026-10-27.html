Today in one paragraph

Two research results and one honest correction. First, we independently rebuilt the statistics behind the 2026 reassessment of the Planet Nine orbital-clustering evidence and reproduce all nine of its published numbers - so the analysis itself can now be checked here, end to end, against public data. Second, we tested whether four published infrared "possible Planet Nine" sightings fit the best-known published model of where the planet should be; one does not fit anywhere the model covers, two sit at its edge. The correction: one of our own stress tests initially looked like it weakened the 2026 evidence; on auditing the method it does not, and we say exactly why below.

1. The 2026 clustering evidence, rebuilt and checked number by number

The original Planet Nine case is that the orbits of distant small bodies point in suspiciously similar directions. A May 2026 reassessment (Siraj, Chyba & Tremaine 2026) found that with the latest object sample the signal is about 1.9 sigma - interesting, not convincing. We re-implemented their three clustering statistics from the paper's own definitions and test them against every number the paper publishes: all nine anchor values match to within 0.05 sigma and 2 degrees (validation notes). We also reproduced the paper's uncertainty interval on the fitted clustering direction: we get 149 degrees (+35.0/-58.5) against the published 151 (+34/-59).

Why this matters: a 1.9-sigma claim lives or dies on details of the computation. Until now, anyone wanting to check it had to trust one codebase. Ours is a second, independent implementation, publicly runnable, agreeing with the original - and it is the tool every later test on this site uses. One caution we now state everywhere: the fitted direction is where the small-body orbits cluster, not a position on the sky for any planet.

2. Do the published infrared sightings fit the model's map?

Four candidate points have been published from far-infrared satellites: a pair from IRAS (1983) and AKARI (2006/07) by Phan et al. 2025, and two single AKARI detections from a 2025 search. We took the 100,000 orbit samples in the published Brown & Batygin (2021) reference population, removed the ones published telescope searches (ZTF, DES, Pan-STARRS) flag as already excluded, wound the rest back to each sighting's own date, and asked: did any surviving model orbit sit there then?

Answer: for the IRAS/AKARI pair, the nearest model orbit in the published ensemble is about 21 degrees away at both dates - roughly 100 times the typical spacing between model points, so it lies far outside the region the published model actually covers. For the two single AKARI points, the nearest model orbits are 2-4 degrees away, similar to gaps at the model's own edges, so we can only say "near the fringe", nothing stronger.

What this does not mean: it does not show the sightings are wrong. It compares them against one 2021 model, and the 2026 evidence says that model is itself too narrow. We also initially overstated this as "outside the model"; on re-check we measured how tightly the 100,000 points actually cover the sky and softened the wording to match what a finite sample can prove. That correction is part of the public record (technical write-up).

3. The correction: a stress test that did not show what it first seemed to

The 2026 method places each small body where a survey actually detected it. We deliberately moved those input positions along each object's orbit - to the middle and end of its observed arc, a median shift of about 11 degrees - and measured what changed: the headline statistic moved from 1.9 sigma to 1.4, and the fitted direction from 150 to 178 degrees.

Our first read was wrong. It looked as if the published result depended on a processing convention. But the shifted positions are synthetic inputs - no telescope detected these objects there, and the paper's method is explicitly built around real detection geometry - so the shifted numbers are not an alternative answer and they neither weaken nor strengthen the published 1.9 sigma. What remains is a bare measurement: inputs moved by this much, outputs changed by that much (full table and limits in the research notes). We keep it public because the correction itself is the useful result: it shows this class of test cannot be used to argue the 2026 evidence up or down.

What we did not do today

No new object discoveries, no telescope time, no claim that Planet Nine does or does not exist, and no settling of whether any infrared sighting is real. A separate check of the candidate positions against the catalogue of known Solar System objects is in progress but not yet validated, so we report nothing from it yet.
