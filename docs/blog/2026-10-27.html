Today in one paragraph

One replication and one exploratory comparison. First, the statistics behind the 2026 reassessment of the Planet Nine orbital-clustering evidence have been independently rebuilt here, and all nine of its published numbers are reproduced - so the analysis can now be checked end to end against public data. Second, an exploratory comparison: the historical infrared candidate positions were compared with a finite sample of orbits from one published model. This comparison does not establish the model's full boundaries or identify or exclude any candidate.

1. The 2026 clustering evidence, rebuilt and checked number by number

The original Planet Nine case is that the orbits of distant small bodies point in suspiciously similar directions. A May 2026 reassessment (Siraj, Chyba & Tremaine 2026) found that with the latest object sample the signal is about 1.9 sigma - interesting, not convincing. The paper's three clustering statistics have been re-implemented here from its own definitions and tested against every number it publishes: all nine anchor values match to within 0.05 sigma and 2 degrees (validation notes). The paper's uncertainty interval on the fitted clustering direction is also reproduced: 149 degrees (+35.0/-58.5) against the published 151 (+34/-59).

Why this matters: a 1.9-sigma claim lives or dies on details of the computation. Until now, anyone wanting to check it had to trust one codebase. This site now carries a second, independent implementation, publicly runnable, agreeing with the original - and it is the tool every later test here uses. One caution: the fitted direction is where the small-body orbits cluster, not a position on the sky for any planet.

2. Comparing the infrared candidate positions with one published model's orbit sample

Four candidate points have been published from far-infrared satellites: a pair from IRAS (1983) and AKARI (2006/07) by Phan et al. 2025, and two single AKARI detections from a 2025 search. The comparison takes the 100,000 orbit samples in the published Brown & Batygin (2021) reference population, removes the ones published telescope searches (ZTF, DES, Pan-STARRS) flag as already excluded, winds the rest back to each sighting's own date, and asks: did any surviving model orbit sit there then?

Answer: for the IRAS/AKARI pair, the nearest model orbit in the published ensemble is about 21 degrees away at both dates - roughly 100 times the typical spacing between model points. Within this finite sample it sits outside the region the sampled orbits cover; this comparison does not establish the model's full boundaries. For the two single AKARI points, the nearest model orbits are 2-4 degrees away, similar to gaps at the sample's own edges, so the strongest available statement is "near the fringe", nothing more. The comparison does not identify or exclude any candidate.

What this does not mean: it does not show the sightings are wrong. It compares them against one 2021 model, and the 2026 evidence says that model is itself too narrow. Correction (27 October 2026): an earlier version of this comparison described the pair as "outside the model". That overstated what a finite orbit sample can show; the wording above reflects the measured coverage of the sample (technical write-up).

3. Correction: a stress-test comparison that was not a valid test

Correction (27 October 2026): an earlier version of today's diary reported that shifting each small body's input position along its orbit (a median shift of about 11 degrees) moved the headline clustering statistic from 1.9 to 1.4 sigma and the fitted direction from 150 to 178 degrees, framed as a stress test of the 2026 result. The shifted positions are synthetic inputs that no telescope observed, and the paper's method is built around real detection geometry, so the shifted numbers are not an alternative measurement and neither weaken nor strengthen the published 1.9 sigma. The numbers are retained in the research notes as a record of what changed and why.
